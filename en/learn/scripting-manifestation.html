Scripting: How to Start and What to Write

Five rules, six opening lines, and the honest state of the evidence · Updated August 4, 2026

Scripting is easy to explain and hard to start. Write your life as though the wish already landed. Simple enough. Then you sit in front of the page and realize the first sentence is the entire problem.

So this guide starts at the first sentence. Then the five rules, six opening lines you can copy word for word, and a second paragraph almost every other guide leaves out, even though that is the part with real research behind it.

What scripting is

You do not pick a wish. You pick a scene. Not „I want the job" but the Tuesday morning where you already have it. Not „I want more calm" but the coffee on the balcony before anyone needs anything from you.

You write that scene in first person and in present tense, as though it were happening. With details you could see and hear, and with the feeling that goes with it.

Where it comes from, briefly and honestly: scripting grew out of the manifestation scene in the 2010s, philosophically out of Neville Goddard and the law of attraction literature. No single originator can be identified.

The five rules

1. A scene, not a goal

A goal is a category. A scene is a moment. Pick a slice you could measure in minutes: a waking up, a conversation, the moment something arrives. Anything longer than an afternoon turns back into a list while you write it.

2. I, and now

„I am sitting", not „I will be sitting". Present tense is the best known rule of the whole technique, and it has a good practical reason: in future tense the text stays an announcement, in present tense you have to decide what it actually looks like.

3. Two senses, minimum

What is there to see, what to hear, how does the floor feel underfoot. A text without senses stays a claim. Two senses are enough, you do not need all five.

4. Name the feeling, not just the circumstance

„I have the apartment" is a circumstance. „I am standing in the doorway and realize that for the first time in years I am not asking anyone whether it is okay" is a feeling. The circumstance is interchangeable. The feeling is yours.

5. No negations

„Finally no more roommates", „no more fighting", „never that office again". Every one of those writes down the thing you are leaving. Flip it and describe what is there instead.

One example, twice

This is how most people write it the first time:

„I am finally self employed and have enough clients. I do not have to go into that office and nobody tells me what to do. I am free and happy."

And this is the same thing as a scene:

„It is 9:15 and I am still in a sweatshirt. The laptop is on the kitchen table, second coffee next to it. I read the email where she says she is happy with the draft, and I feel my shoulders drop. Someone rides past on a bike outside. I have nothing on today that I did not choose myself."

The second one is not prettier, it is more specific. And you can check it: a time, a place, a sound, a physical reaction, a named feeling. That specificity is the one rule that runs through the researched writing exercises, from gratitude to processing hard experiences.

Six opening lines to copy

If the start is the problem, take a start that is already written. Copy the line and keep going without planning ahead.

  • The classic, your day five years out. „I wake up, and the first thing I see is..."
    Keep going with: Where do you wake up? Who or what is around you? How do you spend the morning?
  • When the big picture feels too far away. „Today is an ordinary day, and that is exactly why I love it:..."
    No lottery win, no world trip: the best Tuesday your current life could produce.
  • Before a conversation you are dreading. „I take one breath, and then I say it:..."
    Keep going with: What do you say at the core? How does the other person respond at best? How do you feel walking home?
  • When something specific is pending. „Thank you. It actually happened, and here is how:..."
    A thank you letter for something that has not arrived. How did you find out, who did you call first?
  • When you doubt you have come anywhere. „You are not going to believe me, but..."
    A letter to the person you were three years ago. What were you afraid of then, and what came of it?
  • When you are waiting to be recognized. „I see what you have done, and I am telling you now:..."
    The praise you are waiting for, written by you. What was harder than it looked from outside?

Half a page is enough. Twenty minutes puts you roughly at the length the studied writing exercises use.

The second paragraph almost everyone leaves out

This is where this guide parts ways with most others, and not out of taste. Out of an inconvenient set of findings.

Gabriele Oettingen and Doris Mayer ran four studies in 2002: graduates entering the job market, students with a crush, students facing an exam, and patients recovering from hip surgery. The same pattern showed up in all four: the people with the most positive fantasies about the future had the worse real outcomes. Fewer job offers, lower starting salaries, fewer relationships started, slower recovery.

That is not a reason to drop the picture. It is a reason not to stop there. After the scene, write two more sentences:

  • What is in the way? As concretely as you can. Not „my fear" but „I have not written a resume in two years and have no idea what one looks like now".
  • When and where is the first step? With a time and place inside the sentence. „When I open the laptop tomorrow, I spend twenty minutes on the resume before I read email."

That second sentence is called an implementation intention in the research. The hardest numbers in this guide sit behind it.

What the research shows: The hardest numbers in this guide. Gollwitzer and Sheeran found a clear effect on follow through across 94 independent tests with more than 8000 people in 2006. A newer review from 2024 across 642 tests confirms the direction across a wide range.

Important: If the thing in the way is outside you, it stays outside you. Two jobs, an illness, an agency, missing money. Then the wish gets smaller, not the person guilty. An honestly named external circumstance is actually an excellent trigger for the when then sentence, because it is predictable.

What is supported, and what is not

Where scripting comes from: the manifestation scene, not the lab. What has been studied about it is in the next two boxes, and it is more than most people expect.

The researched cousin: Psychology calls the nearest relative the Best Possible Self exercise. Laura King studied it in 2001 with 81 students over four days, and a meta analysis across 29 studies with 2909 participants finds measurable effects on mood and optimism. That exercise is exactly the first opening line above.

Present tense: doctrine, not a measurement. That present tense outperforms future tense is a rule of the scene, not a measured factor. It is still useful, because it forces you to get specific.

Handwriting or typing: Plenty of guides insist on paper. For what journaling is meant to do, no study compares hand to keyboard. Write where you will actually write, and that is usually the device already in your hand.

When scripting is the wrong tool

About other people. No scene where a specific person changes their mind, their feelings, or their decision. That is the line every serious guide draws.

In grief and in crisis. The scene of the healed life can land as mockery in weeks like that. Free writing about what is actually going on is the better tool then.

As a substitute for treatment. Never. A journal is company.

On days when no sentence about you will hold. There is an exercise that claims nothing about your future: you write about one single value that matters to you, and why. It is the best supported building block in the whole field and it can never feel too big. It is covered in What to write in a manifestation journal.

How often

The studied writing exercises work with four days in a row, twenty minutes each. Not a law, but a useful anchor against the idea that a journal has to be daily and forever.

More useful than any rule is a fixed slot in the day. Timing decides whether you keep going more often than content does.

Ten minutes, starting now

Take a wish that feels close. Take one of the six opening lines. Write half a page, present tense, two senses, one named feeling. Then two sentences underneath: what is in the way, and when you take the first step.
